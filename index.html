Which low-CTR pages stay low? Ranking pages for title and meta review

Samy Elnagar · FlyRank ML Internship, Machine Learning track · Lane: CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring

Abstract

A content team can only review so many pages, and a page with a low click-through rate (CTR) in one month may simply be noise. I asked which visible pages that sat below their peers' CTR in March 2026 were still below their peers in April 2026. I built the label from April and the features from March, and compared a simple baseline (the March CTR gap alone) with gradient-boosting models, scoring every method on clients it had never seen. In this dataset the baseline alone was strong (AUC 0.764), and a model that adds search features to the March gap beat it in all 5 of 5 client-grouped folds (AUC 0.803, Precision@500 0.877). The result is a ranked review queue for a human reviewer; it is an observation from one anonymized dataset, not evidence of why a page's CTR is low or that a rewrite will raise clicks.

1. Introduction

SEO teams often have thousands of visible pages and limited review time. A common first step is to flag pages whose CTR is low for their search position. The decision this paper supports is narrower: which flagged pages deserve a human's time first? A page that stays below its peers month after month is a better candidate than one that dipped once.

CTR depends strongly on position, so each page is compared only with pages in the same position bucket (0–3, 3–10, 10–20). The output is a ranked list with an action label, not a causal claim.

2. Data

The data is the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (pseudonymized, built from Search Console exports; build flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703). I used one table, fact_content_daily_performance, where one row is one day for one page of one client. Features come from March 2026 and the label from April 2026, both mid-panel months. The final month (June 2026) was kept sealed and was not used.

I kept rows with search data, and pages with at least 100 impressions and an average position of 20 or better in both months. That leaves 62,166 pages from 41 clients. I excluded GA4 engagement columns (only about 4.2% of March rows have them), anything from April as a feature, and all raw URLs, queries, titles and client names, which are not in the release.

3. Methodology

  • Features (March only): impressions, average position (impression-weighted), days seen, position volatility, position bucket. A second model also uses March CTR and the March CTR gap.
  • Label: 1 if the page's April CTR is below the median CTR of pages in its April position bucket, else 0. This is true for about half the pages by definition, so the base rate is 0.5 in every bucket. My first version included pages beyond position 20, where the median CTR is zero and the label was always 0; the model could then have learned “worse rank = 0” instead of CTR stability, so I removed those pages.
  • Baseline: rank pages by the March CTR gap alone (median CTR of the page's March bucket minus its own CTR).
  • Models: gradient boosting (HistGradientBoostingClassifier, depth 4, 150 iterations, seed 42).
  • Validation: 5-fold GroupKFold grouped by client, so every client is in exactly one test fold. All methods use the same folds. Metrics: ROC AUC and Precision@500.
  • Leakage checks: March features and April label do not overlap; no product flags, no trend flags, no GA4. Known small leak: bucket medians were computed on all eligible pages, including held-out clients.

4. Results

  • The baseline alone is strong: a page below its peers in March tends to still be below them in April.
  • The model without any CTR feature is weaker than the baseline on AUC. Its Precision@500 difference (0.780 vs 0.766) is within the fold-to-fold spread, so I do not call it better.
  • The model with the March gap beat the baseline in 5 of 5 folds (AUC gain +0.031 to +0.055; Precision@500 gain +0.068 to +0.184).

The winning model uses the baseline's own signal as a feature, so it is best read as “the baseline plus extra features”. Winning 5 of 5 folds is a consistency check, not a significance test.

5. Limitations

  • No causation, no claims about Google. A page below its peers is a candidate for review, not proof that its title is weak. Layout of the results page, intent mismatch or branded searches can also lower CTR.
  • The label is relative. “Below the median” is true for about half of pages by construction.
  • Survivor selection. Only pages that stayed visible (100+ impressions, position 20 or better) in both months are included.
  • Small slice. 41 clients and a single March-to-April pair; no seasonality or long-term trend can be seen. Clients differ in size and start date.
  • Zero-click pages. About 32% of eligible pages have an April CTR of exactly 0, which counts as below peers. I cannot tell whether that means no clicks or unrecorded clicks.
  • Search data only. No engagement or session analysis.
  • Validation limits. Small leak from bucket medians (above); the sealed June month was not scored.
  • A ranked list is not an experiment. Whether a review raises clicks would need a proper test.

Action playbook

  1. review_first, position 3 or better: check the results page for that query (layout, ads, snippets) first, then the title and meta description.
  2. review_first, other positions: read the title and meta description against search intent, then rewrite, adjust or keep.
  3. review_next: review after the first group. These pages have a median of only 485 impressions, so confidence is lower.
  4. monitor: no action now; re-score next month.

Zero-click check. 36.9% of review_first pages have a March CTR of exactly 0, against 24.3% of all eligible pages, so zero-click pages are over-represented but not the majority. Among the 47,056 pages with a March CTR above 0, the top 5% by risk had an April label rate of 0.887 against 0.394 for the rest, so the ranking carries signal beyond zero-click pages.
